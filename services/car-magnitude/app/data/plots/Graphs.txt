1. Comparison of Pooling Methods

What is pooling? After FinBERT reads a headline, it produces one vector (a list of 768 numbers) for every single word. But we need just ONE vector to represent the whole headline. "Pooling" is the rule for squeezing all those word-vectors into one. We tested three rules: use the built-in "summary" vector BERT already makes (pooler), average all the words equally (mean), or let the model learn which words matter most (attention).

What we found: The simplest rule — the built-in pooler — won or tied every time, even against the "smarter" attention method.

Say this: "We tested three ways to summarize the headline into one vector. The simplest one, the model's own built-in summary, performed best — adding a fancier method didn't help."



2. Comparison of Prediction Heads

What is a "head"? After pooling, we still need one final small network that turns that vector into an actual number (the predicted CAR). That final piece is called the "head." We tried a simple 2-layer network (MLP) versus a fancier one with built-in gates that can skip steps (GRN).

What we found: Tied. The fancy version didn't beat the simple one.

Say this: "We compared a simple prediction layer against a more sophisticated one with learned gating — they performed the same, so we kept the simpler one."



3. Model Complexity Comparison

What is "complexity" here? This is about how many adjustable numbers ("parameters") each version of the model has to learn. More parameters usually means more computing power needed, and more risk of memorizing the training data instead of learning general patterns.

What we found: Across every comparison — heads, freeze depth, LoRA — bigger/more complex options never clearly won. Sometimes the smallest option won.

Say this: "Across all our experiments, having more parameters never reliably improved results — on our dataset size, simpler was at least as good, and often more efficient."



4. MLP Width/Depth Architecture Sweep (Large Dataset, 3 Seeds)

What is width/depth? "Width" = how many neurons in one layer. "Depth" = how many layers stacked. We tried a small one, our normal one, a wider one, and a deeper one.

What we found: The deeper version got a better error score (RMSE) but a much worse ranking ability (Spearman) — it got better at guessing "typical" values but worse at telling big reactions from small ones. That's a genuine trade-off, not a clean win.

Say this: "A deeper network lowered our error metric but hurt its ability to rank news by importance — since ranking ability is our core evidence, we kept the simpler design."



5. Architecture Search: Fusion Mechanism Comparison

What is fusion? We have two kinds of information: the headline text, and some numbers (like volatility). "Fusion" is how we combine them before making a prediction. We tried simply placing them side-by-side (concat) versus a smarter method where the numbers reshape the text vector itself (FiLM).

What we found: Tied.

Say this: "We tested a more sophisticated way of blending text and numeric data — it performed the same as simply combining them directly, so we kept the simpler approach."



6. Architecture Search: Encoder Freeze Depth

What is "freezing" a layer? FinBERT has 12 stacked layers. "Freezing" a layer means telling it "don't change during training — stay exactly as you were." We tested freezing 0, 4, 6, or 8 of the bottom layers.

What we found: All four options performed the same. But freezing 6 layers uses far fewer resources (39% fewer numbers to train) for the same result.

Say this: "Freezing different amounts of the model made no real difference in accuracy — so we chose the option that trains 39% fewer parameters, for efficiency, not because it scored higher."



7. Hyperparameter Sweep: Volatility Window

What is volatility? How much a stock's price has been jumping around recently. We tested using the last 2, 3, 10, 20, 30, 40, or 60 days of price history to measure it.

What we found: 10 days was clearly the best — not the shortest, not the longest, a genuine sweet spot. Very short windows (2-3 days) were too noisy (barely enough data points to be reliable).

Say this: "We tested seven different lookback windows for volatility and found 10 days was a real, measurable optimum — not an assumption."





8. Hyperparameter Sweep: Event/CAR Window (3-Seed Evaluation)

What is the event window? How many days after news breaks we measure the price reaction over. We tested 1, 2, 3, and 5 days.

What we found: 2 days won — confirmed properly with multiple test runs, after an earlier, less-reliable single test had misleadingly pointed to 3 days.

Say this: "We tested how many days after news breaks we should measure the reaction, and confirmed 2 days works best — importantly, we re-verified this with repeated testing after an initial result turned out to be misleading."




9. Hyperparameter Sweep: Huber Loss δ

What is this? During training, the model needs a "grading rule" for how wrong its guesses are. Huber loss is gentle on small errors and calm about huge outlier errors — but it needs to know where "normal" ends and "outlier" begins (that cutoff is called δ, delta).

What we found: The default setting from the software library (δ=1.0) was actually broken for our data — it never once treated anything as an outlier, because our numbers are much smaller than 1.0. We recalculated δ properly from our own data's spread.

Say this: "We found the default setting for our loss function was essentially never engaging as designed for our data's scale, and fixed it by deriving the correct value directly from our data instead of trusting the default."





10. Encoder Comparison (3-Seed Evaluation)

What is an encoder? The core language-reading engine. FinBERT is trained specifically on financial text; BERT-base is a general-purpose version with no financial specialization.

What we found: Nearly tied on error, but FinBERT was a bit better at ranking (ability to tell big news from small news).

Say this: "We compared our financial-specialized model against a generic one — they were close, with the financial version showing a modest edge, suggesting domain-specific pretraining helps a little, not dramatically."





11. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning: Full Fine-tuning vs LoRA

What is LoRA? Instead of updating the model's full set of weights, LoRA freezes almost everything and adds tiny, cheap "patches" that do most of the adapting. It's a way to train using far fewer resources.

What we found: LoRA used 61 times fewer trainable numbers, and matched on error — but it was clearly weaker on ranking ability. So we kept full fine-tuning for accuracy, but this shows a genuinely efficient alternative exists.

Say this: "We tested a resource-efficient training method that used 61 times fewer trainable parameters — it matched our main model on raw error but was weaker at ranking, so we kept full fine-tuning, while showing the efficient option is a real, viable alternative."




12. Sri Lankan Vocabulary Extension Ablation Study (Null Result)

What's the problem being fixed? FinBERT was trained on international text, so it doesn't recognize Sri Lankan bank names as whole words — it chops them into meaningless fragments (like splitting "HNB" into random letter-pieces).

What we tried: We manually added the 8 most common Sri Lankan bank names as proper, whole vocabulary words.

What we found: No improvement — even in company-specific news, where this fix should have helped most.

Say this: "We identified and fixed a real limitation — the model fragmenting Sri Lankan company names — but testing showed this specific fix didn't measurably improve results, which we report honestly as a tested, negative finding rather than ignoring it."




13. Final Model Performance Across 11 Independent Runs

Why 11 runs? We discovered that running the exact same settings on different days/sessions gave meaningfully different results — likely due to how the GPU hardware handles calculations slightly differently each time. So instead of trusting one lucky run, we combined results from 11 separate training sessions.

What we found: Across all 11 runs, our ranking ability (Spearman) was positive every single time — a small but genuinely consistent signal. Our precision-recall score beat random guessing by 44 to 51 percent, consistently.

Say this: "Because a single run can be misleading, we aggregated 11 independent training sessions. The result was consistent: a modest but real signal, present in every single run, and a 44 to 51 percent improvement over random chance at flagging significant news."




14. LLM Zero-Shot vs FinBERT+MLP Model

What did we test? Whether a free, ready-made AI chatbot (no training, no fine-tuning) could do our exact job — guess the size of a market reaction from a headline.

What we found: The chatbot mostly just guessed numbers close to the overall average, barely varying its answers per headline. Its ranking score was actually negative — meaning it wasn't really reasoning about the news at all — while our model's ranking score was positive.

Say this: "We tested whether an off-the-shelf AI chatbot could already do this task. It could not — its predictions barely varied per headline, and its ranking correlation was negative, meaning it wasn't actually reasoning about the specific news, while our fine-tuned model showed a genuine, consistent positive relationship."
